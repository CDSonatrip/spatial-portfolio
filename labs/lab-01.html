Cholera in Soho Found Coming from a Pump

The question

Which pump was the source of the cholera outbreak in Soho in 1854?

The data

The basemap is a world topographic map from ArcGIS Online. On the top of the basemap is a map by John Snow, a doctor living in London, who mapped the cholera outbreak in Soho in 1854. Snow's map shows the locations of cholera deaths and water pumps in the area. And the entire map is pulished by Esir as a public teaching map. Notice that the data on snow's map, for example, street names, comes from 1854. But the datapoints for the cholera death and water pumps were geocoded with a modern address.

The method

The heat map here was generated from deathes cases using the algorithm of kernel density estimation. I set the bandwidth of the heat map with a unlabeled slider in the mapviewer and I tested three diffferent bandwirths: the smallest, the medium, and the largest to perform the sensitivity check.

What I found

The result from the heat map slightly changes with different bandwidths, 2 of 3 cases clearly points out that the pump at 43 Broadwick Street is the source of the cholera outbreak. When the bandwidth is "medium" and "largest", the pump at 43 Broadwick street is the only pump that is in the hottest area of the heat map. When the bandwidth is "smallest", the pump at 43 Broadwick Street is still in the hottest area of the heat map, but there are also some death cases around the pump at 1 Carnaby Street and 52 Rupert Street. So in this case I don't think it's enough to conclude that the pump at 43 Broadwick Street is the only source of the cholera outbreak.

What this does not show

This map has several limitations.

First, we have assume that all the data is accurate on this map. However, back in 1854, there may be missing deaths or incorrect address.

Second, we have to assume the population is this area in evenly distributed, since what the map reflects is the number of deaths cases instead of the death rate, so area with the highest number of deaths may not necessarily have the highest death rate.

Third, we have to assume that people who got infected will eventually die or at least there exist a constant death rate. Only in this way, plus the evenly distributed population, the number of death cases can reflect the infection rate.

Fourth, we have to assume that a death is informative about the location of exposure because cholera has an incubation period, you don't die immediately after you get infected. Carriers may travel before they die. So the location of death may not be the same as the location of exposure.
